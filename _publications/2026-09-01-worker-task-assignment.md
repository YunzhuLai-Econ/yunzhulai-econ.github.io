Worker-task assignment is a core managerial decision, but managers may lack the information needed to make it effectively. We conduct a randomized field experiment in three garment manufacturing firms in China, covering 42 production teams and 1,130 workers, to study whether providing managers with systematic worker information improves task assignment and firm performance. In one treatment, managers receive reports on several dimensions of workers' efficiency across tasks; in a second treatment, they additionally receive information on workers' stated task preferences. Both treatments increase team efficiency by approximately 10 percent and reduce worker turnover by around 39 percent, with no detectable decline in product quality. Exploring the underlying mechanisms, we find that the intervention substantially improves managers' knowledge along the dimensions of information provided. However, managers use this information selectively: we detect changes in task assignment only along workers' relative efficiency across tasks, with no corresponding improvement in assignment based on other dimensions of worker performance or preferences. The results show that information provision can improve worker-task matching and firm performance, but that the difficulty of weighing multiple dimensions at once may limit how fully managers incorporate the information they learn into complex decisions.

Managers in developing-country manufacturing firms often face substantial challenges in assigning the right workers to the right tasks, in part because they have limited information about workers' relative skills and preferences. We conduct a randomized field experiment in three large garment manufacturing plants in China to study whether providing managers with information about workers improves task allocation and firm outcomes. We provide randomly selected team managers with either reports summarizing workers' efficiency across tasks or reports that additionally include information on workers' task preferences. We find that providing information increases team efficiency by approximately 10 percent and reduces worker turnover within four months by 34–47 percent. Exploring the underlying mechanisms, we find that the intervention substantially improves managers' knowledge of workers, which leads to improved task assignment along the efficiency dimension. However, despite learning about multiple dimensions of worker performance and preferences, managers selectively incorporate this information into their assignment decisions. The results show that information provision can improve worker-task matching and organizational performance, but that managers' ability to process and apply information across multiple dimensions may limit the extent to which broader information gains translate into changes in managerial decisions.
